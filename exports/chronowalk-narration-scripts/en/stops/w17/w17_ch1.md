Stand here in the piazza for a moment... right beside the fountain. That obelisk at its centre is far older than everything around it, older than the fountain, older than the popes, older than Rome's entire imperial history. It was carved for a pharaoh nearly three thousand years ago, for the temple of Ra in Heliopolis... hauled across the sea by an empire obsessed with taking other cultures' sun gods and turning them into civic decorations.

Now look past the water... up at the massive portico. The building looks like a heavy stone weight dropped into the middle of a modern neighborhood.

Look closely at that triangular pediment above the columns. It's blank now, but it wasn't always. Small holes are still visible in the stone, drilled in a pattern that once anchored a large bronze relief... almost certainly an eagle inside a wreath, the standard imperial symbol of victory and divine favor. That bronze is long gone, melted down centuries ago like almost everything else made of metal on this building. Now look just above the pediment, at that plain strip of brickwork before the dome begins. Architectural historians think that stretch is a repair scar. The leading theory is that Hadrian's engineers originally ordered granite columns fifty Roman feet tall for this porch, but somewhere along the supply chain stretching back to Egypt, something went wrong, and forty-foot columns arrived instead. The whole facade had to be redesigned on the fly to make the shorter columns look intentional. It mostly worked. You'd never notice unless someone pointed it out to you. Now you will.

The inscription across the front says Marcus Agrippa built it... general and son-in-law to Augustus. And the text is a historical layer cake. The inscription is genuine. Agrippa's temple stood for over a century, then burned. Rebuilt, then struck by lightning, and burned again. Two fires, two failed rebuilds, and finally the emperor Hadrian started over completely, roughly a hundred and fifty years after Agrippa's death. He raised the building you're looking at right now, from the foundations up. And when he finished it, he left Agrippa's name on the front, rather than his own. It was a habit of his. The most technically sophisticated building in the ancient world... was built by a ruler who refused to sign his work. Whether that was calculated modesty or simple habit... you can decide.

The doors ahead of you are ancient bronze, very possibly the originals, reworked over the centuries, and they still do the job they were cast for. Nearly everything else metal on this building was carted off long ago, much of it by a seventeenth-century pope melting it down for cannons. The Romans had a sour phrase for it... What the barbarians didn't do, the Barberini did.

Now... step through the bronze doors. And the moment you're inside, stop, and look straight up.
