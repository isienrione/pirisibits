Look up.

There is a hole at the top of the dome. Eight point two meters across... open directly to the sky. No glass... no sealant. When it rains, the water falls straight through this room onto the floor - and the floor handles it perfectly... gently domed like the ceiling above it, with tiny drains at the edges, still functioning exactly as designed. The engineers deliberately built this space to let the weather in.

The dome stretches forty-three point three meters across. The distance from this floor to that opening... is also forty-three point three meters. A perfect, invisible sphere fits precisely inside this room... touching the floor, the walls, and the highest ring of that opening. Whether that geometry is a coincidence is a question historians have spent centuries declining to settle.

What is completely certain... is that no concrete dome wider than this one has ever been raised without steel reinforcement. The concrete above you was poured around one-hundred and twenty-five AD. You are standing inside the largest unreinforced concrete dome on earth. It has been working continuously for nineteen hundred years.

The hole... is why.

Closing a dome at this scale creates crushing forces at the crown. The Romans solved it by leaving the crown empty. The concrete gets thinner and lighter as it rises... from heavy travertine at the base, to light brick, to volcanic pumice near the peak - some of it literally light enough to float on water. The dome manages its own weight. That opening is not a window... it is the structural calculation that keeps the ceiling from falling. An absence that holds the whole room together.

And there's a reason nobody has matched it since, and it isn't just the geometry. It's the concrete itself. The Romans mixed theirs with volcanic ash, and researchers studying samples in recent years found small white mineral chunks scattered through it - long dismissed as sloppy workmanship. It turns out those chunks act almost like a self-repair kit. When tiny cracks form and water seeps in, the lime reacts and reseals the gap before it can spread. Modern concrete cracks, water reaches the steel inside, the steel rusts and expands, and the structure fails from the inside out within a century or two. This dome has no steel to rust. It just keeps healing itself.

Now bring your eyes down to the coffers... those receding squares, five rings, one hundred and forty in total. Each ring sits slightly smaller and higher than the last - an optical trick that makes the ceiling soar rather than simply loom. Each coffer is believed to have once held a gilded bronze rosette, so that torchlight would have made the whole ceiling shimmer like a night sky. Every one was pried out and melted down centuries ago. What you're looking at is the honest architecture, with all its jewelry stolen.
