Look down now, at the floor beneath your feet. This pavement is largely original Roman work... colored stone cut and fitted piece by piece. Look at the colors as you walk... deep purple porphyry, quarried from a single remote spot in the Egyptian desert and reserved for emperors. Yellow marble from North Africa. Veined purple-and-white stone from quarries in what's now central Turkey. This floor is a small map of the entire empire, assembled under your feet.

And the columns you passed on the way in were doing the same work overhead. Sixteen massive monolithic pillars of grey and rose granite - not from any Italian quarry. They were cut as single sixty-ton blocks at Mons Claudianus... a barren, isolated site in the eastern desert of Egypt... hauled on sledges across a hundred kilometers of burning sand to the Nile, floated to Alexandria, shipped across the Mediterranean, and barged up the Tiber to this square. The sheer physics of moving a mountain across an ocean... just to prop up a porch roof... tells you everything about the logistical arrogance of Roman power.

Look around the walls at the seven large niches set into the drum, alternating rectangular and rounded. They almost certainly held statues once - gods associated with the planets, or emperors styled as gods; exactly which is one of the questions the archaeology can't fully answer. But the niches aren't only decorative. They're structural. Hollowing the wall at those points lightens the load, while the solid piers between them concentrate strength exactly where the dome needs it. Even the empty spaces in this building are doing engineering work.

So why is this the one that survived? Rome's temples were legally shut at the end of the fourth century, and for two hundred years this building sat in limbo, still standing, still magnificent, still empty of purpose. Then in six-hundred and nine AD, a pope converted it into a church - and carted in twenty-eight wagonloads of bones from the catacombs to consecrate it. That holy inventory changed the rules. Without it, the Pantheon would have been stripped by the cartload like the Forum temples... broken down into common rubble. The new faith simply needed the space - and in taking it, accidentally preserved the single best-kept building the Roman Empire ever produced.

Which is why, centuries later, this room began collecting the dead it holds now. When you're ready... turn toward the large tomb on the right.
