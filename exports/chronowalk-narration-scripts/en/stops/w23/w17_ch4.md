This is Vittorio Emanuele the Second... the first king of a unified Italy... buried here in eighteen-seventy-eight. And look at the tomb itself - you may notice something that doesn't quite add up for a country that hasn't had a king in eighty years... a guard in a dark uniform and white gloves, standing motionless beside it. That is not a state soldier. Italy is a republic, and no government pays for that guard. He belongs to a private volunteer organization founded by monarchists a century ago... people who give up their own time to stand watch over a king the republic itself doesn't ask anyone to guard. They rotate in shifts, in full ceremonial dress, while tourists file past with their phones out. Nobody requires it. They just never stopped.

Across the room lies his son, King Umberto the First, assassinated in nineteen-hundred, and beside him Queen Margherita - yes, the pizza is probably named after her, though food historians still argue about the details. The symmetry of the tombs is perfect. The family's history was not. Umberto's own son, Vittorio Emanuele the Third, is the great shame of the line - the king who handed the government to Mussolini, signed the racial laws, and fled Rome in the night in nineteen-forty-three. When the war ended, Italy abolished the monarchy and exiled the family by law - for decades, no male heir could legally set foot on Italian soil. The debate over whether his bones may ever enter this room... is still running.

Raphael is buried here too. First chapel on your left, and this one is worth actually stopping at. He died on Good Friday, fifteen-twenty, at thirty-seven... and his body was laid out beside his final, unfinished painting, and the sight reportedly moved everyone who filed past. He had asked to be buried here himself - and getting in at all broke fifteen hundred years of tradition. This had been a space for emperors, saints, and kings... not painters. A pope personally approved it, and it quietly changed what Europe thought an artist was allowed to be. His epitaph, composed by a friend, says it in one line... Here lies Raphael - by whom Nature feared to be surpassed while he lived, and when he died, herself feared to die.

And this room kept teaching long after it stopped collecting. In the fourteen-twenties, an architect named Brunelleschi traveled from Florence specifically to study this dome, and carried what he learned home to raise the cathedral dome that made the Renaissance visible. A century later, Michelangelo studied this room while designing Saint Peter's... and refused to make his dome any wider than this one. He built it taller instead. [small pause]  He never tried to beat it.

Stand here one second longer before you go. This one room has been a temple to every god at once... a monument to a general who never wanted a throne... a tomb for kings whose descendants weren't allowed back into the country... and a shrine to an artist who asked to lie in the only building he trusted to still be standing. The opening at the top let in the light... the rain... and nineteen centuries of people rewriting what this space was for.
