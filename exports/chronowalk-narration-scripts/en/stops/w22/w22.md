[softly]  Walk twenty steps on the ancient surface before I say anything else. Feel where the stones dip - those are ruts. [slow]  Everything that ever left Rome for the south rolled through the grooves under your feet.

[unhurried]  The stones aren't even. You'll have noticed that already-the way the basalt blocks have settled at slightly different angles, so walking requires a kind of attention that flat pavement does not. That unevenness is not damage. It is not neglect. Each stone that sits a few millimeters higher or lower than its neighbors has been there for a different length of time, repaired in a different decade or century by someone who was not thinking about history. They were thinking about the road.

[small breath]

This road was built in 312 BC, by order of the censor Appius Claudius Caecus-Claudius the Blind, though when exactly his sight failed him the sources don't agree. The road that carries his name today runs from Rome to Brindisi on the Adriatic coast: roughly five hundred and sixty-three kilometers, southeast through the Italian peninsula to the port where ships departed for Greece and the East. The first-century poet Statius called it the regina viarum-the queen of roads. He was not exaggerating.

[small breath]

By the time Caesar was born, this road was already two hundred and twelve years old. By the time the Republic fell: two hundred and eighty-five years old. By the time Hadrian's tomb was completed-the drum of concrete you walked past to reach this tour's last stop-the road had been in use for four hundred and fifty-one years. The Colosseum is younger than this road by nearly four centuries.

[small breath]

The law that lined this road with tombs was one of Rome's oldest written statutes: do not bury the dead inside the city. The great roads out of Rome became funerary galleries-wealthy families building monuments facing the road because the road was traveled, because visibility was the closest thing to permanence available. You can still see them: the cylinder of the Tomb of Cecilia Metella ahead of you in the middle distance; fragments of others to either side. The inscriptions carved into those stones addressed the passerby in the second person. They still do.

"Stop, traveler. Read this."

[softly]  You are the traveler.

[small breath]

The road has held other things. In 71 BC, after the general Crassus defeated Spartacus's rebellion, ancient accounts report that approximately six thousand captured rebels were crucified along the Via Appia between Rome and Capua-one cross every thirty-five meters for roughly two hundred kilometers. Every traveler on this road in the months that followed passed between them. The road did not change. What was on it changed.

[small breath]

What you are standing on is not a monument that survived. It is a road that was used. Nobody made a decision to preserve the Via Appia. The basalt blocks are harder than most things Rome built with; leaving them in place was always easier than moving them. The road stayed a road because roads, if they are still roads, tend to stay.

The city is behind you-turn, and you can see the Aurelian Wall, the dome of St. Peter's, twenty-one stops' worth of history arranged in the middle distance. In front of you the road goes south. It goes all the way to Brindisi, and the ships from Brindisi sailed east, and eventually it reaches everything Rome thought of as the edge of the world.

The tombs watch from the sides of the road, waiting for someone to stop and read.

[matter-of-fact]  The road is still here.
